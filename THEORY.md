# GAx — the mathematical object

The starting question is deliberately simple:

> A genetic algorithm gives a succession of population distributions. Is there a useful linear transfer object between generations, and what does the whole generational history know about the future?

The cleanest answer is: **yes, after one lift.** For a fixed fitness rule and a Markov mutation kernel, the usual normalized selection–mutation step is a *projectivized positive linear operator*.

## 1. Hidden linearity

Let `X` be a finite genotype space. Write the population distribution at generation `g` as a probability vector

\[
p_g \in \Delta^{n-1}.
\]

Let fitness enter through positive weights

\[
G_i = e^{\beta f_i}>0,
\]

and let `M` be a column-stochastic mutation matrix,

\[
M_{ji}=\Pr(x_i\to x_j).
\]

Selection followed by mutation is

\[
\tilde p_g = \frac{G\odot p_g}{\langle G,p_g\rangle},
\qquad
p_{g+1}=M\tilde p_g.
\]

Define the positive linear operator

\[
\boxed{L=M\,\mathrm{diag}(G)}.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{
p_{g+1}=\frac{Lp_g}{\mathbf 1^\top Lp_g}.
}
\]

The nonlinearity is only the normalization. Lift from probability vectors to unnormalized positive measures `q_g`:

\[
q_{g+1}=Lq_g,
\qquad
p_g=\frac{q_g}{\mathbf 1^\top q_g}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{q_g=L^g q_0.}
\]

This is the first GAx object. A generation sequence is not merely *similar to* a linear dynamical system. In this idealized mutation–selection model, its unnormalized form **is one**.

This is the same positive-operator / Feynman–Kac structure that appears in interacting particle systems and sequential Monte Carlo. A finite-population GA is then a noisy particle approximation to the deterministic distribution dynamics.

## 2. Generations form a Krylov sequence

Because

\[
q_0,\;Lq_0,\;L^2q_0,\ldots
\]

are successive generations, the history lies in the Krylov spaces

\[
\mathcal K_m(L,q_0)
=\mathrm{span}\{q_0,Lq_0,\ldots,L^{m-1}q_0\}.
\]

This makes the original "interpolate generations toward the future" idea precise.

More generations help only while they add a new Krylov direction. Once the cyclic subspace generated by `q_0` saturates, extra noiseless generations add no new linear information about `L` outside that subspace. With finite populations, later generations can even become less informative as diversity collapses.

So the correct answer to "do more generations always make future interpolation better?" is **no**. The useful quantity is not generation count by itself, but the rank/conditioning of the observed Krylov history.

## 3. Perron–Frobenius: GA convergence as mode purification

If `L` is primitive (strictly mixing after some number of steps), Perron–Frobenius gives a dominant eigenvalue `lambda_0>0` and a positive right eigenvector `v_0`.

Generically,

\[
L^gq_0
= c_0\lambda_0^g v_0
+ c_1\lambda_1^g v_1+\cdots.
\]

After normalization,

\[
p_g \to \frac{v_0}{\mathbf 1^\top v_0}.
\]

So an ordinary mutation–selection GA eventually behaves like a **positive-operator mode purifier**. This is not a metaphor: it is the spectral asymptotic of the lifted operator.

The interesting regime is before collapse. If

\[
|\lambda_1|\approx |\lambda_0|,
\]

then subdominant modes decay slowly. Those long-lived modes are a precise mathematical candidate for the "highways" seen in branching searches: metastable alternatives that remain consequential for many generations.

If mutation makes `L` primitive, permanent branching is not expected in the infinite-time limit. Persistent branches require something extra: reducibility, explicit niching, changing fitness, state-dependent mutation, or a history-dependent operator.

## 4. The Wasserstein claim needs a correction

A tempting statement is:

> selection by `e^{beta f}` plus Gaussian mutation is exactly a JKO/Wasserstein gradient flow.

That is **not generally correct for a GA selection step**.

Take a small selection step

\[
S_\varepsilon[\rho]
=\frac{e^{\varepsilon f}\rho}
{\int e^{\varepsilon f}\rho}.
\]

Expanding,

\[
S_\varepsilon[\rho]
=\rho+\varepsilon\rho(f-\bar f)+O(\varepsilon^2),
\qquad
\bar f=\int f\rho.
\]

Thus selection gives the replicator reaction

\[
\boxed{\partial_t\rho=\rho(f-\bar f).}
\]

This is naturally associated with the Fisher–Rao / Shahshahani geometry of probability distributions: probability is reweighted *at the same location*.

Now let mutation be a small Gaussian convolution of variance `2D epsilon`. Then

\[
K_\varepsilon*\rho
=\rho+\varepsilon D\Delta\rho+O(\varepsilon^2),
\]

so mutation gives

\[
\boxed{\partial_t\rho=D\Delta\rho,}
\]

which *is* the classic Wasserstein-2 gradient flow of entropy.

Alternating the two small steps gives the replicator–mutator equation

\[
\boxed{
\partial_t\rho
= D\Delta\rho+\rho(f-\bar f).
}
\]

Compare that with the Wasserstein free-energy/Fokker–Planck flow

\[
\partial_t\rho
= D\Delta\rho-\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\nabla V),
\qquad V=-f.
\]

These are different equations. A Wasserstein potential moves particles through genotype space along `grad f`; GA selection replicates/reweights successful particles where they already are.

So the better geometry is **two-channel**:

- selection: Fisher–Rao / replicator reaction,
- mutation: Wasserstein transport/diffusion.

Unbalanced optimal transport, Hellinger–Kantorovich, and Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao constructions are the natural mathematical neighborhood because they combine transport with local creation/destruction/reweighting. For a normalized GA one should be careful: the mean-subtracted selection term preserves total probability, so the exact single-metric gradient-flow representation depends on the chosen energy and geometry. GAx should test the splitting directly rather than assume one grand free-energy formula.

## 5. Markovian, but possibly in a larger state

If mutation depends on history,

\[
M_g=M(H_g),
\]

then the population alone need not be Markov. But with an augmented state containing the memory variable `H_g`, the process can again be Markov.

The lifted evolution becomes a positive-operator cocycle

\[
q_{g+1}=L_gq_g,
\qquad
L_g=M_g\,\mathrm{diag}(G_g),
\]

hence

\[
q_g=L_{g-1}L_{g-2}\cdots L_0q_0.
\]

Now a single Perron eigenvector is no longer the right object. The deeper tools are products of positive operators, Lyapunov exponents, Oseledets subspaces, projective contraction, and low-rank structure in

\[
\Delta L_g=L_{g+1}-L_g.
\]

That is where the "fan history" version of GAx becomes mathematically distinct from a fixed CMA-style covariance update.

## 6. Why raw directional transfer can fail while operator transfer survives

Suppose two landscapes differ only by a change of coordinates represented by an invertible map `U`. Their lifted operators satisfy a conjugacy relation

\[
\boxed{L_B=UL_AU^{-1}.}
\]

Raw directions rotate: an eigenvector of `L_A` is not directly reusable in landscape B. But conjugacy invariants survive:

- eigenvalues,
- spectral gaps,
- Jordan/minimal-polynomial structure,
- projective contraction rates,
- relative geometry between invariant subspaces.

This explains why "freeze the direction vectors learned on A and use them on rotated B" is expected to fail. The transferable object, if one exists, should be **coordinate-equivariant or conjugacy-invariant**, not a list of absolute vectors.

That gives GAx a stronger amortization question:

> Can we infer a compact representation of the generational operator that transfers under known or learnable coordinate transformations, even when raw successful directions do not?

## 7. The AI bridge

No neuron analogy is required.

The same factorization appears across modern AI/inference:

\[
\text{reweight by score/likelihood}
\quad+\quad
\text{move with a proposal/noise kernel}.
\]

Selection resembles multiplicative weights, Gibbs/Boltzmann reweighting, and Bayesian likelihood weighting. Mutation resembles proposal kernels, diffusion, and particle rejuvenation. Their particle implementation is close to sequential Monte Carlo.

The GAx question is therefore not "can a GA imitate AI?" It is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Can the history of positive search operators be compressed into reusable predictive geometry?}
}
\]

That points toward learned proposals, black-box optimization, particle inference, and meta-optimization without requiring gradients of the objective.

## 8. Immediate gates

**Gate 0 — Hidden linearity.** Verify numerically that normalized selection+mutation equals projectivized `L^g p_0`, and measure the effective Krylov rank.

**Gate 1 — Conjugacy, not raw vector transfer.** Relabel/rotate the state space, verify `L_B=UL_AU^{-1}`, and show that raw vectors move while spectral invariants survive.

**Gate 2 — Finite population.** Replace exact distributions by `N` particles. Measure how accurately the empirical generations reveal the same Krylov subspace and spectral gap as a function of `N`.

**Gate 3 — Changing operator.** Make mutation covariance/history adaptive and measure `rank_eff(Delta L_g)` versus `rank_eff(L_g)`.

**Gate 4 — Forecast before generation.** Infer the action of `L` only from previous generations, predict `p_{g+h}`, then actually run the GA. Compare persistence, velocity, fitted projective operator, and exact-oracle `L`.

**Gate 5 — Branch lifetime.** Relate the observed lifetime of two evolutionary modes to `|lambda_1/lambda_0|`; then deliberately create near-degeneracy and test the prediction.

The repository should stay on this mathematical track. Biology can later borrow whatever survives.

## 9. Persistent highways through a changing operator

The phrase "persistent modes through a changing operator" needs one mathematical correction. For a fixed operator, eigenvectors are natural modes. For

\[
q_{t+1}=L_t q_t,
\]

an eigenvector of `L_t` need not remain meaningful one step later. The relevant object is a **covariant family of subspaces** that is carried forward by the operator product.

Write such a family as

\[
E_k(t).
\]

The ideal covariance condition is

\[
\boxed{L_t E_k(t)=E_k(t+1).}
\]

More realistically there is small leakage into other families. Under the regularity assumptions of the multiplicative ergodic theorem, the asymptotic version is the Oseledets splitting and its Lyapunov exponents. In finite experiments GAx should use finite-time covariant/singular subspaces and not assume the asymptotic theorem applies automatically.

This gives a sharper candidate for a **highway**:

> A highway is not a frozen direction. It is a computational subspace that deforms while remaining approximately covariant under the sequence of operators.

For a time window `T`, define a finite-time growth rate for a vector in family `k`,

\[
\gamma_k(t,T)
=\frac1T\log
\frac{\|L_{t+T-1}\cdots L_t v_k(t)\|}
{\|v_k(t)\|}.
\]

Then routing is naturally a change in **relative finite-time growth rates**. Context `c` does not have to invoke an expert explicitly. It can alter the effective operator so that

\[
\gamma_a(c) > \gamma_b(c)
\]

for one situation and reverse the inequality for another.

The fixed-operator forgetting-time formula becomes the local approximation

\[
\tau_{1/2,k}
\approx
\frac{\log 2}{\gamma_0-\gamma_k}
\]

when the finite-time Lyapunov gap is roughly stationary. Thus Sigh-style forgetting times survive the move from eigenvalues to changing operators.

### Measuring whether a computational highway is real

Let `P_k(t)` project onto computational family `k`, where the family may be identified empirically from counterfactual behavior, Jacobians, activations, or pathway use rather than raw parameter distance.

Three quantities matter:

1. **within-family gain**

\[
g_k(t)=\|P_k(t+1)L_tP_k(t)\|;
\]

2. **cross-family leakage**

\[
\ell_{jk}(t)=\|P_j(t+1)L_tP_k(t)\|,\qquad j\neq k;
\]

3. **family separation**, measured for example by principal angles between `E_j(t)` and `E_k(t)`.

A useful spectral router should produce strong context-dependent contrast in the `g_k` while keeping leakage and family collapse bounded.

This makes the "lethal average" failure precise. If two computational families are replaced by one centroid direction, the principal angle vanishes and counterfactual distinctions disappear even if current-task loss remains low.

### Self-rewriting spectral router

Now let consequences change the operator itself:

\[
L_{t+1}=L_t+\Delta L_t(R_t,H_t),
\]

where `R_t` is consequence/reward and `H_t` is whatever history the algorithm retains.

The self-rewriting-router problem is not merely to increase reward. It is to learn `Delta L_t` such that future contexts acquire the right modal gain **without destroying the distinguishability of the computational families**.

Schematically,

\[
\boxed{
\text{context}
\to L_t
\to \text{relative mode growth}
\to \text{computation}
\to \text{consequence}
\to \Delta L_t
\to \text{future routing changes}.
}
\]

A natural experimental objective is therefore multi-part:

\[
\text{performance}
+\alpha\,\text{routing contrast}
-\beta\,\text{cross-mode leakage}
-\chi\,\text{collapse of subspace angles}.
\]

This is stricter than mixture-of-experts routing. The modes are not required to be fixed modules, and the router is not required to be a separate classifier. The modes may be moving covariant computational subspaces, while history changes the operator that determines their future gains.

Gate 4's neural intervention test supplies the first operational definition of such a computational family: two mechanisms can be behaviorally identical in the observed world yet be distinct because interventions reveal different Jacobian/pathway signatures. The next GAx gates should ask whether those counterfactually distinct families can remain covariant while the routing operator learns.
